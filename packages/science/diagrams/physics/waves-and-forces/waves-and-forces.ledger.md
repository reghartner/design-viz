# Two invisible sums — story and source ledger

## Audience and intended understanding

Introductory physics learners should be able to explain why opening a second
coherent source can darken a point on a screen, and why a puck can move right
while accelerating left. The page uses authored snapshots and calculated panel
readouts. It is a classroom model, not an experimental data set or a trajectory
simulation.

## Source and model boundaries

| Claim in the page | Basis | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Whole wavelength path difference reinforces; half wavelength cancels | [OpenStax, Young's Double-Slit Interference](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/3-1-youngs-double-slit-interference) | The page assumes two in-phase sources of equal amplitude. |
| The screen pattern combines two-source interference and a slit-width diffraction envelope in a physical double-slit setup | [OpenStax, Double-Slit Diffraction](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/4-3-double-slit-diffraction) | This page deliberately omits the diffraction envelope and geometric spreading. The flat one-source strip is a normalized reference, not a prediction for a real slit. |
| Net force is the vector sum of external forces; acceleration points with that sum and equals ΣF/m | [OpenStax, Newton's Second Law](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/5-3-newtons-second-law) | Friction, drag, and vertical support forces are outside the horizontal classroom comparison. |
| Zero net force permits constant nonzero velocity | [OpenStax, Newton's First Law](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/5-2-newtons-first-law) | The later velocity values are authored snapshots, not derived from earlier steps or elapsed time. |

All distances, forces, masses, velocities, and observations on this page are
illustrative. The wave panel computes exact Euclidean path lengths for the
declared scene geometry: d = 4 cm, L = 10 cm, screen height = 12 cm. For two
equal in-phase sources, it uses I/I₀ = 4 cos²(π Δr/λ), where Δr = r₂ − r₁ and
I₀ is one-source intensity. At y = 1.284446 cm, Δr is approximately 0.5 cm;
this gives I/I₀ ≈ 0 for λ = 1 cm and I/I₀ ≈ 2 for λ = 2 cm. Rounding affects
display, not the geometric calculation. The single-source normalized intensity
is 1 across the drawn screen.

Puck A remains 2 kg and Puck B remains 4 kg. With a +4 N horizontal net force,
their accelerations are +2 and +1 m/s² respectively. A later A snapshot sets
velocity to +2 m/s by authorship. Removing the push gives zero acceleration;
a −4 N push gives −2 m/s² while velocity is still +2 m/s. No stop time,
distance, or future velocity is claimed.

## Ideation choices

1. **Selected: two-source wavefield.** Most surprising teaching turn is a
   newly opened source darkening a point. A fixed probe allows controlled
   wavelength and shutter comparisons on independent paths.
2. **Selected: free-body force comparison.** Two permanent mass declarations
   make the inverse-mass relation visible without changing a body's mass in
   flight. The coasting and braking beats address the common force/velocity
   confusion.
3. **Deferred: refraction and critical angle.** Strong geometry, but a narrower
   mechanism and additional boundary cases; it would distract from the two
   complete stories here.
